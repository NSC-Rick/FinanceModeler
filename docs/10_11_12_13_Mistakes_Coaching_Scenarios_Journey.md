# PART 1: COMMON MISTAKES

## Revenue Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Unrealistic Growth Rates
**What It Looks Like**: User sets 50%+ annual growth for established business  
**Why It Occurs**: Optimism bias, pressure to show impressive projections  
**How to Recognize**: Growth rate > 30% for non-startup, no market justification  
**How to Correct**: Use industry benchmarks (5-15% for established), create conservative/moderate/optimistic scenarios  
**Eric's Response**: "Growth above 30% is very aggressive. Can you support this with market data? Let's model a conservative case (10%) and optimistic case (25%) to show range of outcomes."

### Mistake 2: Forgetting COGS
**What It Looks Like**: COGS set to 0% for product business  
**Why It Occurs**: Misunderstanding what COGS includes, focusing only on overhead  
**How to Recognize**: COGS = 0% but business sells physical products  
**How to Correct**: Set COGS based on industry benchmarks (retail 50-70%, manufacturing 40-60%)  
**Eric's Response**: "COGS is the direct cost to produce your product. For retail, this is typically 50-70% of revenue. Let's use 60% as starting point and refine from there."

### Mistake 3: Including Overhead in COGS
**What It Looks Like**: COGS > 80% because user included rent, marketing, admin salaries  
**Why It Occurs**: Confusion about what belongs in COGS vs. operating expenses  
**How to Recognize**: COGS percentage unusually high, user mentions "all costs" in COGS  
**How to Correct**: Explain COGS = only DIRECT costs (materials, direct labor, shipping). Move overhead to Operating Expenses.  
**Eric's Response**: "COGS should only include direct costs to produce each unit. Rent, marketing, and admin salaries are operating expenses, not COGS. Let's separate these out."

### Mistake 4: Ignoring Seasonality
**What It Looks Like**: Retail business with even monthly revenue  
**Why It Occurs**: Simplification, not understanding cash flow impact  
**How to Recognize**: Retail/tourism/seasonal business with seasonality = OFF  
**How to Correct**: Enable seasonality, use Retail preset or custom weights  
**Eric's Response**: "Retail businesses typically see 30-40% of annual revenue in Q4 (holidays). This affects your cash flow and working capital needs. Let's enable seasonality to model this accurately."

### Mistake 5: No Startup Ramp
**What It Looks Like**: New business with full revenue from Month 1  
**Why It Occurs**: Optimism, not understanding customer acquisition takes time  
**How to Recognize**: Startup with ramp months = 0, full revenue immediately  
**How to Correct**: Set realistic ramp (3-12 months depending on business type)  
**Eric's Response**: "New businesses don't reach full capacity immediately. Customer acquisition, word-of-mouth, and operational efficiency all take time. Let's model a 6-month ramp to steady-state revenue."

## Payroll Mistakes

### Mistake 6: Forgetting Payroll Taxes
**What It Looks Like**: Payroll tax % = 0% or < 7.65%  
**Why It Occurs**: Not understanding employer tax obligations  
**How to Recognize**: Payroll tax below 7.65% (FICA minimum)  
**How to Correct**: Set to at least 7.65%, typically 8-12% including unemployment  
**Eric's Response**: "Employers must pay at least 7.65% for FICA (Social Security + Medicare). You'll also owe state/federal unemployment and possibly workers comp. Let's use 10% to be safe."

### Mistake 7: Not Planning for Raises
**What It Looks Like**: Annual raise % = 0% for all roles  
**Why It Occurs**: Trying to minimize costs, not thinking long-term  
**How to Recognize**: All roles have 0% raises  
**How to Correct**: Set realistic raises (2-5% annually)  
**Eric's Response**: "Employees expect annual raises, even if just cost-of-living (2-3%). Ignoring this makes your Year 2-3 projections unrealistically low. Let's plan for 3% annual raises."

### Mistake 8: Classifying All Labor as Indirect
**What It Looks Like**: Production workers marked as "indirect"  
**Why It Occurs**: Not understanding direct vs. indirect classification  
**How to Recognize**: Manufacturing/service business with all indirect labor  
**How to Correct**: Classify production workers as "direct" (flows to COGS)  
**Eric's Response**: "Production workers should be 'direct' labor because they're part of COGS. This affects your gross margin, which lenders use to evaluate your business."

### Mistake 9: Double-Counting Owner Compensation
**What It Looks Like**: Owner included in payroll AND owner distribution configured  
**Why It Occurs**: Confusion about how to handle owner compensation  
**How to Recognize**: Owner in payroll roles + owner compensation > 0  
**How to Correct**: Choose one method: salary (payroll) OR distribution (owner comp)  
**Eric's Response**: "You're paying yourself twice—once as salary and once as distribution. Choose one method based on your business structure. S-corp typically uses salary, LLC typically uses distributions."

## Operating Expense Mistakes

### Mistake 10: Forgetting Major Expense Categories
**What It Looks Like**: No insurance, no marketing, no professional fees  
**Why It Occurs**: Focusing only on obvious expenses (rent, utilities)  
**How to Recognize**: Only 2-3 expense categories filled in  
**How to Correct**: Review all 10 default categories, add amounts  
**Eric's Response**: "You're missing some major expenses. Don't forget: insurance (liability, property), professional fees (accountant, lawyer), marketing, and bank/credit card fees. Let's fill these in."

### Mistake 11: Rent Too High
**What It Looks Like**: Rent > 12% of revenue  
**Why It Occurs**: Expensive location, low revenue, not checking benchmarks  
**How to Recognize**: Rent / Revenue > 12%  
**How to Correct**: Negotiate lower rent, relocate, or grow revenue  
**Eric's Response**: "Rent above 12% of revenue is a yellow flag. This limits your profitability. Can you negotiate with landlord, find cheaper space, or grow revenue to bring this ratio down?"

### Mistake 12: Treating Marketing as Optional
**What It Looks Like**: Marketing = $0  
**Why It Occurs**: Trying to minimize costs, "word of mouth will be enough"  
**How to Recognize**: Marketing budget = 0 for new/growth business  
**How to Correct**: Allocate 5-20% of revenue for marketing depending on stage  
**Eric's Response**: "New businesses need marketing to acquire customers. Word-of-mouth takes time. Let's budget at least 10% of revenue for marketing in Year 1, decreasing as you build reputation."

## Financing Mistakes

### Mistake 13: Borrowing Too Much (DSCR < 1.25)
**What It Looks Like**: DSCR below 1.25, often below 1.0  
**Why It Occurs**: Wanting maximum loan amount, not understanding DSCR  
**How to Recognize**: Red flag "DSCR below 1.0" or yellow flag "DSCR 1.0-1.20"  
**How to Correct**: Reduce loan amount, increase revenue, reduce expenses, or extend term  
**Eric's Response**: "DSCR below 1.25 is risky—lenders may not approve. You need $1.25 of EBITDA for every $1 of debt service. Let's reduce the loan amount or improve profitability."

### Mistake 14: Borrowing Too Little
**What It Looks Like**: Negative cash in Month 6-12  
**Why It Occurs**: Underestimating working capital needs, trying to minimize debt  
**How to Recognize**: Cash flow shows negative ending cash  
**How to Correct**: Increase loan amount or reduce expenses  
**Eric's Response**: "You'll run out of cash in Month 8. Undercapitalization is a top reason businesses fail. Let's borrow enough to cover working capital plus 10% buffer."

### Mistake 15: Setting AR Days to 0 for B2B Business
**What It Looks Like**: B2B service business with AR days = 0  
**Why It Occurs**: Not understanding invoicing creates receivables  
**How to Recognize**: B2B business with 0 AR days  
**How to Correct**: Set realistic collection period (30-45 days for B2B)  
**Eric's Response**: "If you invoice customers, you'll have accounts receivable. B2B customers typically pay in 30-45 days. Let's use 30 days to model the cash flow impact."

### Mistake 16: Setting AP Days Too High
**What It Looks Like**: AP days = 90+  
**Why It Occurs**: Trying to maximize cash flow, not understanding supplier terms  
**How to Recognize**: AP days > 60  
**How to Correct**: Use realistic supplier terms (typically 30 days)  
**Eric's Response**: "Most suppliers offer Net 30 terms, some Net 45. Setting AP to 90 days assumes suppliers will wait 3 months for payment—unlikely unless you have strong negotiating power."

### Mistake 17: Ignoring Working Capital
**What It Looks Like**: AR/AP/Inventory all set to 0 for product business  
**Why It Occurs**: Not understanding working capital concept  
**How to Recognize**: Product business with all working capital = 0  
**How to Correct**: Set realistic days based on business model  
**Eric's Response**: "Product businesses need working capital for inventory and customer receivables. Let's set: AR = 15 days (credit cards), Inventory = 45 days (stock on hand). This shows the cash you'll need."

## Review & Analysis Mistakes

### Mistake 18: Confusing Profit with Cash Flow
**What It Looks Like**: "I'm profitable so I won't run out of cash"  
**Why It Occurs**: Not understanding timing differences  
**How to Recognize**: User sees positive net income, ignores negative cash  
**How to Correct**: Explain profit ≠ cash; show working capital impact  
**Eric's Response**: "Profit and cash flow are different. You can be profitable but run out of cash due to: customer receivables (AR), inventory investment, and loan principal payments. Check your Cash Flow Statement."

### Mistake 19: Ignoring Negative Cash Warnings
**What It Looks Like**: Proceeding with plan despite negative ending cash  
**Why It Occurs**: Optimism, not understanding severity  
**How to Recognize**: User sees negative cash, doesn't adjust plan  
**How to Correct**: Emphasize cash is oxygen—business dies without it  
**Eric's Response**: "Negative cash means you'll run out of money. This is critical—you can't operate without cash. You need to: increase starting capital, reduce expenses, or improve revenue."

### Mistake 20: Not Testing Sensitivities
**What It Looks Like**: Only one scenario, no "what-if" analysis  
**Why It Occurs**: Time pressure, not understanding importance  
**How to Recognize**: User saves one scenario and stops  
**How to Correct**: Encourage Modeler use, multiple scenarios  
**Eric's Response**: "What if revenue is 10% lower than projected? Let's test this in Modeler. Lenders want to see you've thought through different outcomes."

# PART 2: COACHING OPPORTUNITIES

## Proactive Teaching Moments

### Opportunity 1: First Revenue Stream Entry
**Trigger**: User adds first revenue stream  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain revenue → COGS → gross profit flow  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Revenue is your top line—what customers pay. COGS is what it costs you to deliver. The difference is gross profit, which covers overhead and generates net profit. Let's make sure your pricing covers COGS plus overhead plus desired profit margin."

### Opportunity 2: COGS Percentage Selection
**Trigger**: User sets COGS percentage  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain gross margin and industry benchmarks  
**Eric's Guidance**: "COGS of 30% means 70% gross margin—for every $100 in sales, $70 is left to cover overhead and profit. Your industry benchmark is [X]%. You're at [Y]%, which is [better/worse]. This affects your pricing power and profitability."

### Opportunity 3: Adding First Employee
**Trigger**: User adds payroll role  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain fully-loaded labor costs  
**Eric's Guidance**: "A $50k salary actually costs $60-65k when you include payroll taxes (7.65%+) and benefits (10-30%). Always calculate fully-loaded cost when budgeting for staff."

### Opportunity 4: High Payroll Tax Entry
**Trigger**: User sets payroll tax > 15%  
**Teaching Moment**: Verify understanding of employer vs. employee taxes  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Payroll tax here is what YOU pay as employer, not what employees pay. Minimum is 7.65% for FICA. Did you mean to include employee withholding? That's separate and not modeled here."

### Opportunity 5: Zero Marketing Budget
**Trigger**: User sets marketing = $0  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain customer acquisition importance  
**Eric's Guidance**: "How will customers find you? Even word-of-mouth businesses need some marketing (signage, website, social media). Consider budgeting 5-10% of revenue for customer acquisition, especially in Year 1."

### Opportunity 6: First Loan Configuration
**Trigger**: User enters loan amount  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain DSCR and lender requirements  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Lenders look at DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) = EBITDA / Debt Service. They require 1.25+ to ensure you can comfortably make payments. Let's check your DSCR in the Review page."

### Opportunity 7: Low DSCR Warning
**Trigger**: DSCR < 1.25 in Review page  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain debt service risk  
**Eric's Guidance**: "DSCR of [X] means you have $[X] of operating profit for every $1 of debt payment. Below 1.25 is risky. Options: reduce loan amount, increase revenue, reduce expenses, or extend loan term."

### Opportunity 8: Negative Cash Flow
**Trigger**: Ending cash goes negative  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain working capital and cash needs  
**Eric's Guidance**: "You'll run out of cash in Month [X]. This is often due to working capital—cash tied up in customer receivables and inventory. You need [Y] in starting capital to avoid running out. This is in addition to your loan."

### Opportunity 9: High Growth Rate Entry
**Trigger**: User sets growth > 30%  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain growth challenges and capital needs  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Growth above 30% is exciting but challenging. It requires: marketing investment, working capital (AR/inventory), operational capacity, and management bandwidth. Make sure you have resources to support this growth."

### Opportunity 10: Seasonality Enablement
**Trigger**: User enables seasonality  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain cash flow implications  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Seasonal businesses need more working capital during peak months and have cash shortfalls in slow months. Plan for: inventory buildup before peak, seasonal hiring, and cash reserves for slow periods."

### Opportunity 11: Working Capital Configuration
**Trigger**: User sets AR/AP/Inventory days  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain working capital cycle  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Working capital is the cash cycle: you buy inventory (cash out), sell to customers (AR created), collect payment (cash in), pay suppliers (AP settled). Longer cycle = more cash tied up. Let's optimize this."

### Opportunity 12: Capital Stack Planning
**Trigger**: User enables capital stack (Advanced mode)  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain uses vs. sources balance  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Capital stack shows where money comes from (sources) and where it goes (uses). They must balance. Uses: purchase price, working capital, closing costs. Sources: buyer equity, bank loan, seller note. Let's make sure these match."

### Opportunity 13: First Scenario Save
**Trigger**: User saves first scenario  
**Teaching Moment**: Encourage multiple scenarios  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Great! You've saved your base case. Now consider creating: Conservative Case (lower revenue, higher expenses), Optimistic Case (higher revenue, lower expenses). Lenders like to see you've thought through different outcomes."

### Opportunity 14: Modeler First Use
**Trigger**: User opens Modeler page  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain sensitivity testing  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Modeler lets you test 'what-if' scenarios without changing your base model. Try: What if revenue is 10% lower? What if expenses increase 5%? This helps you understand your business sensitivity and prepare for different outcomes."

### Opportunity 15: Deal Optimizer First Use
**Trigger**: User opens Deal Optimizer  
**Teaching Moment**: Explain optimization objectives  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Deal Optimizer finds the best capital structure for your goals. Want to minimize your down payment? Maximize purchase price? Optimize DSCR? Set your objective and constraints, and it'll search thousands of combinations to find the best option."

# PART 3: SCENARIO PLANNING

## Scenario Types

### Base Case Scenario
**Purpose**: Most likely outcome based on realistic assumptions  
**Characteristics**:
- Moderate growth (10-20% for growth business, 5-10% for established)
- Industry-standard COGS
- Competitive pricing
- Realistic working capital
- Adequate staffing

**When to Use**: Primary scenario for lender presentations, planning  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Your base case should be realistic and defensible. Use industry benchmarks, market research, and conservative assumptions. This is what you'll be measured against."

### Conservative Case Scenario
**Purpose**: Worst-case planning, stress testing  
**Characteristics**:
- Low growth (0-5%)
- Higher COGS (efficiency issues)
- Lower pricing (competitive pressure)
- Slower collections (AR days +10)
- Higher expenses

**When to Use**: Risk assessment, contingency planning, lender sensitivity analysis  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Conservative case shows you've thought through downside risks. If this scenario still works (positive cash, DSCR > 1.25), your business is resilient."

### Optimistic Case Scenario
**Purpose**: Best-case outcome, upside potential  
**Characteristics**:
- High growth (25-50%)
- Lower COGS (economies of scale)
- Premium pricing (strong brand)
- Faster collections (AR days -10)
- Operating leverage (expenses grow slower than revenue)

**When to Use**: Showing upside potential, attracting investors  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Optimistic case shows upside potential if things go well. But don't base your plan on this—use it to show range of outcomes."

### Sensitivity Scenarios

#### Revenue Sensitivity
**Test**: Revenue ±10%, ±20%  
**Purpose**: Understand revenue impact on profitability and cash  
**Eric's Guidance**: "If revenue is 10% lower, are you still profitable? Do you still have positive cash? This shows how sensitive your business is to revenue fluctuations."

#### COGS Sensitivity
**Test**: COGS ±5%  
**Purpose**: Understand supplier pricing or efficiency impact  
**Eric's Guidance**: "If supplier costs increase 5%, how does it affect your margins? Can you pass this to customers through pricing, or do you absorb it?"

#### Expense Sensitivity
**Test**: Operating expenses ±10%  
**Purpose**: Understand cost control importance  
**Eric's Guidance**: "If expenses are 10% higher than planned, what happens to cash flow? This shows importance of cost discipline."

#### Growth Rate Sensitivity
**Test**: Growth at 5%, 15%, 25%  
**Purpose**: Understand growth impact on cash needs  
**Eric's Guidance**: "Higher growth requires more working capital. At 25% growth, you need $[X] more in starting capital than at 5% growth. Make sure you're capitalized for your growth plans."

### Industry-Specific Scenarios

#### Retail Scenario
**Key Assumptions**:
- Seasonality enabled (Retail preset)
- COGS: 50-70%
- Inventory days: 45-90
- AR days: 5-15 (mostly credit cards)
- Marketing: 5-10% of revenue

#### Restaurant Scenario
**Key Assumptions**:
- COGS: 28-35% (food cost)
- Inventory days: 3-7 (perishables)
- AR days: 0-5 (mostly cash)
- High direct labor (kitchen staff)
- Startup ramp: 6-12 months

#### Professional Services Scenario
**Key Assumptions**:
- COGS: 10-25% (mostly direct labor)
- Inventory days: 0 (service business)
- AR days: 30-45 (B2B invoicing)
- High indirect labor (consultants, admin)
- Marketing: 10-20% (client acquisition)

#### Manufacturing Scenario
**Key Assumptions**:
- COGS: 40-60%
- Inventory days: 60-120 (raw materials + finished goods)
- AR days: 30-60 (B2B terms)
- High direct labor (production workers)
- Startup ramp: 12-24 months

### Acquisition vs. Startup Scenarios

#### Startup Scenario
**Key Settings**:
- Business Stage: Startup
- Model Mode: Startup
- Working Capital Source: Buyer Injected
- Startup Ramp: 3-12 months
- Opening balances: All zero

**Eric's Guidance**: "Startup mode assumes you're building from zero. No opening AR/AP/Inventory. Revenue ramps gradually. You'll need more starting capital to fund early losses."

#### Acquisition Scenario
**Key Settings**:
- Business Stage: Acquisition
- Model Mode: Acquisition
- Working Capital Source: Seller Provided or Buyer Injected
- Startup Ramp: 0 (existing revenue)
- Opening balances: Calculated from assumptions

**Eric's Guidance**: "Acquisition mode assumes you're buying existing business with current revenue. Working capital balances transfer from seller. Less startup risk, but you're paying for existing cash flow."
